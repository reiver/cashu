Cashu

private digital cash for Bitcoin, built on blind signatures

by Charles Iliya Krempeaux

What is Cashu‽

The most succinct way I can think of to explain what Cashu is is:

🠶 digital cash that the issuer cannot trace

A bit more precisely — Cashu is an open protocol for Chaumian ecash on top of Bitcoin. A server, called a mint, takes in bitcoin (usually over the Lightning Network) and hands out ecash tokens. Those tokens can be passed from person to person, like paper cash. And, when someone eventually brings a token back to the mint, the mint can tell that the token is real — but it cannot tell who it originally gave that token to.

That last part is the magic. And, it comes from a cryptographic trick called a blind signature.

But that is a lot of jargon. So, before getting technical, let's build an intuition for what Cashu is without any of it.

Explained For A 12 Year Old

Imagine there is a school fair. At the school fair, you cannot pay for things with regular money. Instead, you go to the ticket booth, give them some regular money, and they give you fair tickets. Then you use those fair tickets to buy popcorn, play games, and so on.

(In Cashu, the ticket booth is called the mint. The fair tickets are called ecash. And, the regular money is bitcoin.)

Here is the problem. If the ticket booth wrote down a serial number for every ticket, and wrote down who they gave each ticket to — then later, when the popcorn stand turned the tickets back in, the ticket booth would know exactly who bought popcorn, and when. Every ticket would be a little spy.

So, this fair does something clever.

The Secret Number

When you want a ticket, you (not the ticket booth) make up a long secret number, and write it on a blank slip of paper. Something like 83920174650283746… — so long that no one else will ever pick the same one.

The Magic Envelope

Next, you put your slip of paper inside a special envelope. The envelope is lined with carbon paper. So, if anyone stamps the outside of the envelope, the stamp also comes through onto the slip of paper inside.

You hand the sealed envelope, plus your regular money, to the ticket booth. The ticket booth stamps the outside of the envelope with its special stamp (one that no one else has), and hands it back. The ticket booth never opens the envelope. So, it never sees your secret number.

You walk away, open the envelope, and take out your slip. Now your slip has your secret number on it, and the ticket booth's official stamp. That stamped slip is your ticket.

Spending The Ticket

You give your stamped slip to the popcorn stand. The popcorn stand can give it to someone else, who can give it to someone else, and so on — just like cash.

Eventually someone takes the slip back to the ticket booth to trade it in. The ticket booth looks at the stamp, and says, “yes, that's our stamp, this ticket is real.”

But the ticket booth has no idea who it first gave this ticket to. It only ever saw envelopes. It never saw the secret number until right now. So, it can't connect this ticket to you.

No Copying

“Wait,” you might say, “can't I just photocopy my ticket and spend it twice?”

Nope. The ticket booth keeps a list of every secret number that has been traded in. If the same secret number shows up a second time, the ticket booth says, “sorry, that one has already been used.”

So, the ticket booth doesn't keep a list of who has tickets. It only keeps a list of secret numbers that are already used up.

The Catch

There is one big catch. You have to trust the ticket booth.

If the people running the ticket booth pack up and run away with all the regular money, your tickets become worthless pieces of paper. So, you should only get tickets from a ticket booth you trust, and you probably shouldn't keep a huge pile of tickets for a long time.

That's Cashu

And that is basically Cashu.

  • The ticket booth is the mint.
  • The fair tickets are ecash tokens.
  • The regular money is bitcoin.
  • The secret number is the secret.
  • The carbon-paper envelope is blinding.
  • The stamp is the mint's signature (made with the mint's private-key).
  • Opening the envelope is unblinding.
  • The list of used-up secret numbers is the spent list.

The rest of this article is about how this actually works — with math (in place of envelopes), and computers (in place of a ticket booth).

Ecash

Cashu is a type of ecash. More specifically, Chaumian ecash — named after cryptographer David Chaum, who invented it.

Chaumian ecash has the following characteristics:

bearer asset

Whoever holds the token owns it. Like paper cash. There are no accounts. (And, like paper cash, if you lose it, it is gone.)

issued by a mint

A single party, the mint, issues the tokens, and redeems them. The mint holds the backing money.

unlinkable

Because of blind signatures, the mint cannot link the token it issued to the token that is later redeemed.

custodial

You must trust the mint to honor the tokens it issued.

Cashu takes this 40+ year old idea, and backs the ecash with bitcoin.

Terminology

Some terms that come up a lot when talking about Cashu:

mint

The server that issues and redeems ecash. It holds the bitcoin. It has private-keys that it uses to sign (and later verify) ecash. In the Cashu specifications, the mint is called Bob.

wallet

The software, on the user's device, that holds ecash, and talks to mints. In the Cashu specifications, the sending user is called Alice, and the receiving user is called Carol.

proof

A single piece of ecash, of a single denomination. A proof is basically a secret plus the mint's (unblinded) signature on that secret.

token

A bundle of one or more proofs, plus the mint's URL, serialized into a string (that starts with cashuA or cashuB) so it can be sent to someone. (People often use “token” loosely to mean any amount of Cashu ecash.)

minting

Turning bitcoin into ecash. The user pays the mint (e.g., pays a Lightning invoice), and gets ecash.

melting

Turning ecash back into bitcoin. The user gives the mint ecash, and the mint pays out bitcoin (e.g., pays a Lightning invoice).

swapping

Trading ecash for new ecash at the same mint. This is how you make change, and how a receiver makes sure the sender can't spend the ecash again.

sat

Short for satoshi. The smallest unit of bitcoin. 1 bitcoin = 100,000,000 sats. Most Cashu mints count in sats.

NUT

The Cashu specifications are called NUTs — short for “Notation, Usage, and Terminology”. (The NUT-00 document itself says “Notation, Utilization, and Terminology”.) They are numbered: NUT-00, NUT-01, NUT-02, etc.

(The Cashu project is full of nut puns. The specifications are NUTs. The reference implementation is Nutshell. There is a yearly hackathon called NutNovember. A Cashu wallet's key-derivation path even uses the number 129372, which is the Unicode code point for the peanut emoji 🥜 (U+1F95C).)

History

The core idea behind Cashu is much older than Bitcoin.

History: David Chaum

In 1982, David Chaum presented the paper “Blind Signatures for Untraceable Payments” at the Crypto 82 conference. (It was published in the conference proceedings in 1983, so you will see it cited as both 1982 and 1983.)

Chaum's paper introduced blind signatures — a way for a bank to sign something without seeing what it is signing. That is what makes untraceable ecash possible. Chaum's construction used RSA:

History: DigiCash

Chaum founded DigiCash in 1989 to commercialize his idea, under the name eCash. In 1995, the Mark Twain Bank of St. Louis, Missouri became the first bank to offer eCash. Banks in other countries licensed it too — including Deutsche Bank, Credit Suisse, and others.

But, it didn't catch on. DigiCash filed for bankruptcy in 1998. Calle (the creator of Cashu) summed it up as: DigiCash failed and “PayPal and Visa took over.”

History: David Wagner

RSA was patented at the time. So, in 1996, David Wagner posted “Chaumian ecash without RSA” to the cypherpunks mailing-list. Wagner's version replaced RSA with a blinded Diffie-Hellman key exchange.

Ben Laurie later implemented Wagner's scheme in a library called Lucre. But, for the most part, the idea sat mostly forgotten for about 25 years.

History: Ruben Somsen

In February 2022, Ruben Somsen published a write-up called “Blind Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange (blind ecash)”, which restated Wagner's “seemingly long forgotten” scheme using elliptic-curve notation — the same kind of cryptography Bitcoin uses.

This is the scheme Cashu is built on.

History: Calle

Cashu was created by a pseudonymous developer who goes by Calle (callebtc), who previously worked on LNbits. The first commit of Nutshell (the reference implementation of Cashu, written in Python) is dated 11 September 2022. Cashu was publicly launched in October 2022. The NUTs specification repository was created in January 2023.

Cashu describes itself as having “no company, no token, no treasury”. The specifications and reference implementation are open-source, under the MIT license.

The Three Operations

At a high level, a Cashu wallet does 3 things with a mint:

mint

Pay the mint bitcoin. Get ecash.

swap

Give the mint ecash. Get new ecash of the same total value (minus any fee).

melt

Give the mint ecash. The mint pays bitcoin to wherever you tell it.

Notice what is not on the list — sending. Sending ecash to another person does not involve the mint at all. Alice just gives Carol the token (as text, a QR code, NFC, a chat message, etc). Carol then (usually immediately) does a swap with the mint, so that Alice can no longer spend it.

Blind Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange

The cryptography at the heart of Cashu is called Blind Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange (BDHKE). It is specified in NUT-00.

It uses the secp256k1 elliptic-curve — the same elliptic-curve that Bitcoin uses.

(If you are not familiar with elliptic-curve cryptography, here is what you need to know for this article: Lower-case letters (k, r) are big secret numbers, called scalars. Upper-case letters (K, Y, C) are points on the curve. G is a fixed, well-known point that everyone agrees on. You can add points together, and you can multiply a point by a number. Going from k to k×G is easy, but going backwards — from k×G back to k — is practically impossible.)

(In the Cashu specifications, B_ is called the blinded message, C_ is called the blinded signature (or “promise”), and C is called the unblinded signature. The underscore in B_ and C_ is a stand-in for a prime mark (B′, C′) that is easy to type.)

Blind Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange: Why Is It Called That

In a regular Diffie-Hellman key exchange, Alice and Bob each have a private-key (a and k), and they each publish a public-key (a×G and k×G). They can then both compute the same shared secret, k×a×G, without ever sending it.

Steps 2 and 3 above have that same shape — Alice sends a point, and Bob multiplies it by his private-key k. Except that Alice's point is blinded (by r). And, unusually, Bob sends the “shared secret” (C_) back to Alice. Hence Blind Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange.

Blind Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange: hash_to_curve

The secret x is just bytes. (NUT-00 recommends 32 random bytes, written as a 64-character hexadecimal string.) To do elliptic-curve math with it, it needs to be turned into a point on the curve. That is what hash_to_curve does:

Why bother with hashing‽ Why not let the user pick Y directly‽

Because, if a user could choose Y freely, they could choose Y = y×G for some y they know. And then they could compute k×Y = y×K themselves — using only the mint's public-key. I.e., they could forge ecash.

By deriving Y from a hash, nobody knows the y for which Y = y×G. So the only way to get a valid C = k×Y is from the mint.

Blind Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange: Why The Mint Cannot Link

When the mint signs, it sees only B_ = Y + r×G. Because r is a random number, B_ is just a random-looking point. It reveals nothing about Y (or x).

When the ecash is later spent, the mint sees x and C. But it has never seen those before. And, without r, it cannot connect (x, C) to any of the (B_, C_) pairs it signed in the past.

So, the mint cannot tell which minting created the ecash that is now being spent.

Blind Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange: Not Quite A Signature

Notice that verifying C requires computing k × hash_to_curve(x) — which requires the mint's private-key k. So, only the mint can check whether ecash is valid. That makes C different from a normal digital signature, which anyone can check with a public-key.

(People pointed this out about Wagner's scheme back in 1996.)

Cashu partly fixes this with DLEQ proofs (NUT-12), covered further below.

Double-Spending

A proof is just data. Data can be copied. So, what stops someone from spending the same proof twice‽

The mint keeps a list of every proof that has been spent. More precisely, it keeps a list of Y = hash_to_curve(x) values. (These are sometimes called nullifiers.) When a proof is spent, the mint checks:

  1. is the signature valid (i.e., k × hash_to_curve(x) == C)‽
  2. is Y not already on the spent list‽

If both are true, it accepts the proof, and adds Y to the spent list.

Note that the spent list is not a list of users, or balances. It is just a list of used-up secrets. The mint does not know who has how much ecash.

A consequence of this is that, when Carol receives ecash from Alice, Carol should swap it right away. Until Carol swaps it, Alice still knows the secrets, and could spend them first. As NUT-03 puts it, only after Carol has redeemed new outputs can Alice no longer double-spend.

NUT-07 lets a wallet ask a mint about the state of a proof (by its Y) — which is one of: UNSPENT, PENDING, or SPENT.

Denominations: Making Change

What if you have the wrong denominations‽ You swap.

Here is the example from NUT-03. Alice has proofs worth [32, 16, 16] (a total of 64), and wants to send Carol 40. She swaps them with the mint for [8, 32, 8, 16] (still a total of 64). Then she sends Carol 32 + 8 (= 40), and keeps 8 + 16 (= 24).

(The specification recommends a wallet sort the outputs it asks for by amount, so the order doesn't hint at which ones are for the payment.)

Keysets

A mint's set of public-keys — one per denomination — is called a keyset. A keyset looks something like this:

A mint can have more than one keyset (for different units, like sat or usd, or because it rotated its keys). Each keyset has a keyset ID, which is derived by hashing its public-keys (and some of its settings). Every proof says which keyset ID it belongs to.

A wallet fetches the mint's keysets over HTTP, at GET /v1/keys and GET /v1/keysets (NUT-01 and NUT-02).

The original keyset IDs were 8 bytes, starting with 00 (e.g., 009a1f293253e41e). Since January 2026, the current version is 33 bytes, starting with 01. The change came after a security researcher (conduition) found that a malicious mint could craft a colliding keyset ID under the old scheme, and use it to steal ecash a user held at other mints.

Wallets are supposed to compute keyset IDs themselves, rather than trusting what the mint says. That also helps catch a mint that tries to give different users different keys, so it can tell them apart (sometimes called “tagging”).

Tokens: Bearer

Remember, a token is a bearer asset. The token string is the money. Anyone who sees it can spend it. If you paste a token into a public chat, whoever swaps it first gets it.

Lightning

Cashu mints usually connect to the Bitcoin world via the Lightning Network. Bitcoin comes in to the mint as a Lightning payment, and goes out of the mint as a Lightning payment.

Both minting and melting use a two-step quote pattern: first ask the mint for a quote, then act on it.

Lightning: Fee Reserve

Paying over Lightning costs a routing fee. But the mint doesn't know exactly how much the fee will be until after it pays. So, it asks for a fee_reserve up front (the maximum it might need), and gives back whatever it didn't use as change (NUT-08).

But how can the mint give change as blinded ecash, when it doesn't know how much change there will be until afterwards‽ The trick is that the wallet sends some blank outputs — blinded messages without an amount. The mint fills in whichever amounts it needs. The wallet sends max(ceil(log2(fee_reserve)), 1) blank outputs, which is enough to represent any amount up to the fee_reserve.

Here is the example from NUT-08. To pay a 100,000 sat invoice with a fee_reserve of 1,000 sats, the wallet sends 101,000 sats of proofs, plus 10 blank outputs. The actual Lightning fee turns out to be 100 sats. So, the mint returns 900 sats of change, using 4 of the blank outputs:

Lightning: Moving Between Mints

Ecash from one mint can only be redeemed at that mint. To move value from mint A to mint B, a wallet melts at mint A, to pay a Lightning invoice that came from a mint quote at mint B.

Lightning: And Others

The quote pattern is generic. The payment method is part of the URL (e.g., /v1/mint/quote/bolt11). So, besides BOLT11 Lightning invoices (NUT-23), there is also BOLT12 (NUT-25), and, since 2026, plain on-chain Bitcoin addresses (NUT-30).

Spending Conditions

So far, the secret x has been just random bytes. Whoever knows x (and C) can spend the ecash.

The mint sees this structured secret only at spending time, when it checks the conditions.

Spending Conditions: Pay To Public Key

Pay To Public Key (P2PK, NUT-11) locks ecash to a public-key. To spend it, you need a Schnorr signature from the matching private-key.

This is useful because Alice can send Carol ecash that only Carol can spend — and that even Alice can no longer spend. Which makes it much safer for Carol to receive ecash while offline.

Tags allow for multi-signature (pubkeys, n_sigs), a time-lock (locktime), and refund keys (refund) that can spend it after the time-lock expires.

Pay-to-Blinded-Key (P2BK, NUT-28) is a 2026 addition that blinds the public-key, so the mint can't tell that different P2PK payments went to the same person.

Spending Conditions: Hashed Timelock Contracts

Hashed Timelock Contracts (HTLCs, NUT-14) lock ecash to a hash. To spend it, you need the preimage of that hash (i.e., the value that hashes to it). After a time-lock expires, the sender can take it back.

This is the same building block that the Lightning Network uses. It makes atomic swaps possible (for example, between two mints).

Spending Conditions: A Warning

NUT-10 warns that if a mint does not support a particular kind of spending condition, it may treat the ecash as a regular anyone-can-spend proof. So, a wallet should check what a mint supports (NUT-06, GET /v1/info) before locking ecash at it.

DLEQ Proofs

Earlier, it was mentioned that only the mint can verify ecash, since that requires its private-key k. That is a problem for Carol. If Alice hands Carol a token while Carol is offline, how can Carol know the mint really signed it‽

NUT-12 solves this with a discrete-log equality (DLEQ) proof. When the mint returns C_ = k×B_, it also returns a small zero-knowledge proof (e, s) that the same k was used in K = k×G (its published public-key) and in C_ = k×B_.

When Alice sends Carol a proof, she includes the DLEQ values plus her blinding factor r. With those, Carol can rebuild B_ and C_, and check the proof using only public information.

Note, though, that a DLEQ proof only shows that the ecash was signed by the mint. It does not show that the ecash hasn't already been spent. Only the mint knows that. (Which is why DLEQ plus P2PK is a nice combination for receiving offline.)

(And, the wallet must strip r out before sending the proof to the mint — since r is exactly the thing that would let the mint link the ecash back to its minting.)

Backup And Restore

Since ecash is a bearer asset, losing your wallet means losing your ecash.

NUT-13 lets a wallet generate its secrets x and blinding factors r deterministically, from a 12-word seed phrase (like a Bitcoin wallet), plus a counter. And, NUT-09 has the mint remember every (B_, C_) pair it ever signed.

So, to restore, a wallet regenerates its B_ values from the seed phrase, asks the mint for the matching C_ values (POST /v1/restore), unblinds them, and then checks which are still unspent.

(Of course, this only works if the mint is still around.)

HTTP 402 Payment Required

Way back in 1997, HTTP/1.1 reserved the status code 402 Payment Required — “for future use”. It never really got used, because the Web never got a native way to pay.

NUT-24 uses Cashu to fill that gap. A server responds with 402 Payment Required, and an X-Cashu header containing a payment request (how much, in what unit, from which mints). The client retries the request, with a cashuB token in the X-Cashu header.

Since a Cashu token is just a string, and doesn't require an account, this is a natural fit for pay-per-request APIs. For example, Routstr is a marketplace for LLM (AI) inference paid for with Cashu.

Nostr

Cashu and Nostr have become closely connected.

NIP-60 — Cashu Wallets

Stores a Cashu wallet (encrypted) on Nostr relays, so it follows the user from app to app. Unspent proofs are kept in kind 7375 events, the wallet itself is a kind 17375 event, and spending history is kind 7376.

NIP-61 — Nutzaps

A nutzap is a Cashu payment (P2PK-locked to the recipient) published as a Nostr event (kind 9321). The payment is its own receipt. And, thanks to DLEQ proofs, anyone can check it offline.

NIP-87 — Ecash Mint Discoverability

Mints can announce themselves (kind 38172), and users can recommend and review them (kind 38000).

Cashu payment requests (NUT-18) can also use Nostr direct messages to deliver the payment.

Trust And Risks

Back to the catch from the school fair. Cashu is custodial. The Cashu documentation says it plainly: Ecash wallets are always custodial.

Calle has said: when a mint goes down, your sats are gone.

Some of the risks:

rug pull

The mint operator could disappear with the bitcoin.

getting rugged

The mint itself could be hacked, or drained via a software bug. (For example, in June 2026, a researcher showed how one of Nutshell's Lightning backends could be tricked into paying excessive routing fees, again and again.)

going offline

If the mint is down, you cannot swap or melt. And, a receiver cannot safely accept ecash without swapping.

fractional reserve

Nothing stops a mint from secretly issuing more ecash than it has bitcoin. (“Proof of liabilities” schemes have been proposed, but none are part of the specification as of November 2026.)

censorship

Since only the mint can verify ecash, a mint could falsely claim something was already spent. And, a mint can see the Lightning invoices it is asked to pay, and refuse some of them.

lost wallet

Bearer asset. No seed phrase backup means no recovery.

The usual advice is: use mints you trust, keep amounts small, and don't sit on ecash for long. Think of it like the cash in your pocket, rather than your savings.

(Mint directories such as cashumints.space list mints, with reviews. It warns: Mints are custodial. A mint can disappear with your money.)

Privacy

Cashu's privacy is from the mint. But it isn't total.

What the mint cannot see:

  • which minting a spent proof came from (thanks to blinding),
  • anyone's balance (there are no accounts),
  • who sent ecash to whom (tokens are passed directly between users).

What the mint can see:

  • the amounts (denominations are public),
  • the Lightning invoices it pays on a melt (and so, who is being paid),
  • your IP address (unless you use Tor, or similar),
  • timing (e.g., a 1,337 sat mint followed by a 1,337 sat melt a minute later is suspicious).

Also, the more people using the same mint (with the same keyset), the bigger the crowd you hide in.

Authentication

Some mint operators want to (or must) restrict who can use their mint. So, in 2025, two optional specifications were added:

NUT-21 — Clear Authentication

Log in with OAuth 2.0 / OpenID Connect. (The specification itself warns that this “breaks the user's privacy”.)

NUT-22 — Blind Authentication

Once logged in, the user mints blind auth tokens — which are themselves ecash (with unit auth) — and spends one on each request. So, the mint knows a request came from someone it authorized, but not who.

These were controversial. Some argued that standardizing authentication makes KYC and censorship easier. Others replied that authentication is optional, and users can pick other mints.

Cashu versus Fedimint

Fedimint is another Chaumian ecash system for Bitcoin. The big difference is who holds the bitcoin.

The two have been moving closer together. In October 2026, at bitcoin++ in Berlin, Calle presented “Federated Cashu” — a Cashu mint run by several operators, with threshold keys, and a move to BLS signatures (in what are being called “v3 keysets”). As of November 2026, that is still a proposal, and not yet part of the specifications.

The NUTs

As of November 2026, there are 31 NUTs. NUT-00 through NUT-06 are mandatory. The rest are optional. A mint lists the optional NUTs it supports at GET /v1/info.
